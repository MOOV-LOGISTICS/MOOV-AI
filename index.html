The fifth category

There is a layer underneath this that we cannot see

Beyond the four tools in this table, staff also reach for free consumer AI — Doubao, the DeepSeek web app, the assistant built into DingTalk. These cost us nothing, which is exactly why there is no account list, no usage record and no way to know what has been pasted into them.

This is the real argument for a sanctioned front door. The question is not only what we spend on AI; it is how much of our AI use we can actually see. Today the honest answer is: the four columns below, and nothing else.

Findings

Three things stand out

Finding 01

Spend is concentrated, and volatile

Qoder is ¥22,979 of a ¥49,790 monthly total — 46 % of AI spend on 42 of 154 seats, at ¥547 per user per month. That is 2.4× Copilot and 2.9× Gemini.

The monthly figure also swings hard: ¥3,162 in April, ¥48,870 in July. 73 % of it is credit consumption, not licences — so cutting seats would barely move it. A usage gate would.

Finding 02

We run two overlapping office assistants

Gemini (76 seats) and Copilot (36 seats) do substantially the same job in two different office suites. Together that is 112 seats and ¥22,727 a month — 46 % of the total, the same share as Qoder.

There may be good reasons: the suites are separate purchases and the populations may not overlap. But at renewal it should be a decision, not a default. IT can produce the actual usage split.

Finding 03

Only one platform gets cheaper as it grows

Gemini, Copilot and Qoder all bill by the user, so their per-user cost is fixed by construction — ¥189, ¥232 and ¥547 a month, whatever we do.

MOOV AI is a fixed ¥4,083 a month plus tokens. Across our 300 staff that is ¥13.61 each before tokens and ¥14.32 all-in; at 500 people it falls to ¥8.87. Rolling it out to more people lowers its unit cost instead of raising the bill.

03 / Qoder Work

What Qoder Work actually is

Qoder Work is Alibaba's agentic work platform, and it carries two capabilities at once: software development and office output. Unlike an assistant that answers in a chat window, it takes a task, plans the steps, executes it — writing and running code, or producing the document — and reports back. It can also read and act on files on the user's own computer. That is why it is the most capable tool we have — and why it is metered by consumption rather than by seat.

What we run on it
  • MOOV OS development — the platform's code is written largely with Qoder
  • Operational automation — recurring jobs handed to an agent rather than scripted by hand
  • Prototyping — standing up a working version of an idea before committing engineering time

Why it matters here

Nearly three-quarters of Qoder spend is usage, not seats. Cutting licences would barely move the number; the only effective controls are who is allowed to consume credits and an alert when consumption crosses a threshold.

The corollary: work that does not need an agentic coding platform should not be done on one. That is the case for a cheaper baseline layer underneath it — which is what MOOV AI is for.

Dify alone

A developer toolbox

  • Basic permissions that do not map to a company hierarchy
  • Every user reaches every knowledge base directly

Same engine, different control, different audience. Business teams get a guided front door; developers keep the full Dify workspace. The split is what keeps low-value build work out of the IT queue without giving up control of what reaches production.

05 / Decision

The four tools are not competing for the same job. Ordered by cost per user, they form a natural pyramid: the cheapest layer should reach everyone, and the most expensive layer should be the one we qualify people for.
